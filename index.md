I study how neural systems compute and organize information across time, from circadian rhythms unfolding over days to the millisecond structure of a single action potential.

I moved from Spain to the United States to pursue research opportunities and an interdisciplinary curriculum. As an undergraduate at Smith College, working with Mary Harrington, I developed a non-invasive method for recording circadian bioluminescence from freely behaving mice using PER2::LUCIFERASE reporters, along with a computational pipeline for extracting rhythm parameters from the resulting time-series data, published in the *Journal of Biological Rhythms*.

I'm now a PhD candidate in Neurosciences at UC San Diego, co-mentored by Bradley Voytek, whose lab works on algorithms and data science for neural signals, and Alysson Muotri, whose lab builds and records from human cortical organoids. My dissertation asks what the shape of an action potential, not just whether it fires, reveals about the state of a neuron and the circuit around it. The standard model treats spikes as binary, all-or-nothing events; my work shows that waveform shape varies systematically with extracellular oscillatory state and with causal stimulation, structure that the binary model discards. I've coauthored seven peer-reviewed publications and given ten conference presentations, including at SfN, FENS, and CNS.

Alongside that central question, I build analysis pipelines for high-density MEA recordings and organoid stimulation and recording protocols, including experiments on how psychedelics modulate network plasticity, and I study neuronal timescales as an organizing principle for how circuits integrate information across developmental stages.

Outside the lab, I enjoy reading, learning languages, and working out. I've also danced since childhood and am currently a competitive ballroom dancer.

I'm defending in Fall 2026. My [resume](/resume/) has full details on publications, presentations, and research experience.
